# Pilot Batch 01 State-Selection Memo

## Project

**American Multilingual Publics and the Digital State**

## Pilot domain

**Medicaid / public health coverage**

## Pilot states

- Minnesota
- Washington
- Maryland
- Texas
- Michigan

## Purpose of the selection

Pilot Batch 01 uses a **purposive maximum-variation design**, not a representative sample of the United States. The goal is to test whether the project's data infrastructure can capture meaningful differences in multilingual public-benefit communication, digital service delivery, translation practice, AI or machine-translation use, human oversight, and communicative administrative burden across contrasting state environments.

The five states were selected because, taken together, they offer variation in language-access infrastructure, digital public-service systems, immigrant and refugee communication needs, state administrative arrangements, and political context. The pilot is intended to stress-test the collection framework before any larger-scale expansion.

## State cases

### Minnesota

**Primary agency/program:** Minnesota Department of Human Services (DHS), Medical Assistance and MinnesotaCare.

Minnesota is a particularly useful case for the project's initial Amharic and Afaan Oromo focus. DHS already provides some health-care application materials in Afaan Oromo, and state benefit infrastructure includes multilingual assistance in both Amharic and Oromo. Minnesota therefore allows the pilot to examine a setting where multilingual access is not merely hypothetical but is already visible in public-facing benefit communication.

Minnesota also provides an opportunity to compare different forms of multilingual provision within the same state, including translated applications, translated notices, multilingual assistance lines, and digital benefit interfaces.

**Initial evidence to track:**
- Medical Assistance and MinnesotaCare application materials;
- translated notices;
- multilingual benefit-navigation pages;
- interpreter and language-assistance statements;
- renewal, eligibility, cancellation, and documentation communications;
- any use of browser translation, machine translation, or AI-assisted public communication.

### Washington

**Primary agency/program:** Washington State Health Care Authority (HCA), Apple Health (Medicaid).

Washington offers a comparatively developed language-access environment. HCA publicly documents free interpreter and translation services for Apple Health clients, provides multilingual materials, and maintains explicit guidance on limited-English-proficiency services. Public-facing Apple Health materials include Amharic among supported languages in several areas.

Washington is especially valuable for the human-oversight dimension because state materials document qualifications for interpreters and translators and distinguish written translation from oral interpretation. This makes it a strong case for examining how language-access policy becomes operational practice.

**Initial evidence to track:**
- Apple Health application and renewal materials;
- eligibility and coverage-change information;
- interpreter and translation policies;
- translated notices and multilingual flyers;
- language-access statements;
- provider-facing requirements regarding qualified language access;
- any documented use of AI or machine translation and any associated review process.

### Maryland

**Primary agency/program:** Maryland Department of Health, Maryland Medicaid.

Maryland adds a strong multilingual and immigrant-serving case in the Washington metropolitan region. State and local health-system materials provide free language assistance and public-facing multilingual notices, including Amharic in some contexts. Maryland Medicaid managed-care rules also require access to written material in prevalent non-English languages and oral interpretation in non-English languages.

Maryland is useful for examining the relationship between statewide Medicaid communication, managed-care requirements, local health-department implementation, and multilingual public communication.

**Initial evidence to track:**
- Maryland Medicaid eligibility and application materials;
- managed-care member communications;
- translated notices and language-assistance statements;
- appeal, denial, renewal, and coverage-change information;
- local versus statewide implementation differences;
- documented translation and review practices.

### Texas

**Primary agency/program:** Texas Health and Human Services Commission (HHSC), Texas Medicaid.

Texas provides an important contrast case because of its scale, linguistic diversity, decentralized service environment, and highly digital benefit infrastructure. HHSC policy explicitly addresses interpreter services and translated written materials for applicants and recipients with limited English proficiency.

Texas is also analytically useful because public-benefit communication frequently intersects with immigration, eligibility, fraud, and deservingness discourse. The state therefore offers a strong setting for examining how institutional language access operates within a politically salient information environment.

**Initial evidence to track:**
- Your Texas Benefits and Medicaid eligibility materials;
- application and redetermination communications;
- translated eligibility notices;
- interpreter and translation-service requirements;
- fraud and identity-verification messages;
- digital portal language options;
- any AI, automated translation, or machine-translation use and associated safeguards.

### Michigan

**Primary agency/program:** Michigan Department of Health and Human Services (MDHHS), Michigan Medicaid.

Michigan contributes institutional and geographic variation to the pilot. MDHHS publicly documents bilingual interpreter services and provides translated Medicaid notices and forms in selected languages. Michigan also offers a useful setting for examining how statewide benefit communication interacts with immigrant and refugee communities across different local contexts.

Michigan is particularly useful as a comparison with Minnesota and Washington because it has documented language-assistance mechanisms but a different administrative and communication environment.

**Initial evidence to track:**
- Medicaid application and eligibility materials;
- translated notices and forms;
- interpreter-service policies;
- renewal, denial, and appeal communications;
- noncitizen eligibility communications;
- digital benefit interfaces and language options;
- any documented machine-translation or human-review process.

## Comparative dimensions

Pilot Batch 01 will compare states along the following dimensions:

1. **Language availability** — Which languages are supported, and for which communication objects?
2. **Depth of access** — Are translations available only for general information, or also for high-consequence material such as eligibility, renewals, denials, appeals, deadlines, and documentation requirements?
3. **Digital access** — How well are multilingual materials integrated into websites, portals, downloadable documents, and other digital channels?
4. **Translation method** — Human translation, staff translation, vendor translation, browser translation, machine translation, AI-assisted translation, or unknown.
5. **Human oversight** — Whether review is documented, who reviews, and whether high-risk content receives stronger quality assurance.
6. **Information integrity** — Accuracy, completeness, currency, consistency, source authority, and actionability.
7. **Communicative administrative burden** — How much effort users must expend to find, interpret, verify, and act on benefit information.
8. **Institutional transparency** — Whether agencies disclose translation methods, vendors, AI/MT use, quality-control procedures, or correction mechanisms.

## Unit of analysis

The primary unit is a **public-benefit communication item** rather than a state policy.

Examples include:
- eligibility web pages;
- application instructions;
- renewal instructions;
- translated forms;
- appeal-rights statements;
- adverse-action notices;
- fraud or scam warnings;
- identity-verification messages;
- language-access statements;
- portal messages;
- chatbot or automated-help responses.

## Initial sample target

For the first pilot, target approximately **10–15 communication items per state** within the Medicaid/public health coverage domain.

This yields an initial English-source corpus of approximately **50–75 items**, before counting multilingual versions.

Priority should be given to high-consequence communication involving:
- eligibility;
- application requirements;
- documentation;
- renewal;
- termination or cancellation;
- denial;
- appeal rights;
- deadlines;
- identity verification;
- fraud or scam warnings.

## Language strategy

The data structure should record all publicly available languages, but the first deep-evaluation track will prioritize:

- **Amharic**
- **Afaan Oromo**

A state is not excluded simply because one or both languages are unavailable. Non-availability is itself analytically relevant and should be documented.

## Selection logic and limits

The five states are not intended to represent all U.S. states statistically. They are selected to maximize institutional and communicative variation while providing strong conditions for testing the project's initial theoretical and methodological framework.

No state should be coded as having or lacking a practice solely because public documentation was or was not found. The dataset must distinguish:

- documented practice;
- no public documentation found;
- unknown practice;
- researcher inference.

The first pilot should be treated as a methodological and conceptual test. Any national-scale claims should wait until the sampling frame is substantially expanded.

## Stopping rule for Pilot Batch 01

Collection for a state can stop when:

1. the major high-consequence Medicaid communication categories have been sampled;
2. no new communication type or language-access workflow is appearing in successive records;
3. the target of roughly 10–15 analytically useful items has been reached; and
4. each collected item has sufficient provenance to enter the v1.0 collection template.

If fewer than 10 suitable items are available, the shortfall should be documented rather than filled with weak or redundant records.

## Decision

Any change to this state set after collection begins should be documented as a protocol change rather than made silently.
